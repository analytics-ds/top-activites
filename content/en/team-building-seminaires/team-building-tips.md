A team building rarely fails because of the activity. It fails because the objective was never set, the budget showed up too late, one colleague could not take part, or the team ran off at 6.30pm without exchanging three words. Here are the 10 points to settle before booking anything, in the order they actually come up.

## 1. Start from the objective, never from the activity

This is the most common and most expensive mistake. You stumble on an activity that looks fun, you book it, and afterwards you wonder why nobody mentioned it again.

Three common objectives call for three very different formats:

- **Onboarding new joiners**: you need forced mixing, so reshuffled teams and an activity where people have to talk to make progress.
- **Connecting two departments that ignore each other**: you need cooperation under pressure, not competition between the two camps, otherwise you widen the gap instead of closing it.
- **Rewarding an intense period**: no cohesion goal here, just enjoyment. A fun activity and a good meal are enough, and definitely no disguised workshop.

Write your objective in one sentence before opening a single search engine. If you cannot, you are not ready to book.

## 2. Set the budget per person before you start looking

Searching without a budget guarantees you fall in love with an 8,000-euro quote that management will turn down. Three tiers structure the Paris market:

| Format | Budget per person | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Activity only (1 to 2 hours) | €25 to €40 | The activity, no catering |
| Activity plus meal or cocktails | €50 to €80 | A full evening in a single venue |
| Full day | €100 and up | Several sequences, venue buyout, catering |

Two habits that genuinely lower the bill: aim for a weekday slot rather than a Friday night, and pick a venue that charges **per lane, per box or per room** rather than per head, since the cost per person collapses as soon as you fill it. Our worked examples are in the [fun bars and venues](/en/bars-lieux-ludiques/) section.

## 3. Count the real headcount, not the theoretical one

Out of 40 invitations there will be holidays, travel, sick leave and last-minute drop-outs. Booking exactly 40 places means risking paying for 40 and hosting 31.

More importantly, check the **capacity per session**, not just the venue capacity. Many activities run in small groups. Bomb Squad, for instance, plays with 2 to 6 people per one-hour session, so a group of 30 means several parallel sessions or a rotation, with someone managing the slots. A venue advertising "up to 150 people" does not necessarily have them playing at the same time.

The right question to ask: how many people play **simultaneously**, and what do the others do in the meantime?

## 4. Pick a format where nobody sits on the bench

A very physical activity sidelines anyone with a health condition, a pregnancy, an injury, or simply no wish to sweat in front of colleagues. A very verbal activity excludes anyone not fully comfortable in the working language.

Questions to ask before confirming:

- Is there a real physical requirement, and is there a non-physical role in the game?
- Is the venue accessible to people with reduced mobility?
- Are dietary requirements handled properly, not just "we'll see on the day"?
- Does the activity work without perfect command of the language?

A mixed format, where you can be useful without being the fastest, always brings more people in than a purely sporting one.

## 5. Book far earlier than you think

Weekday evening slots, Tuesday to Thursday, are the ones companies want most, and they are exactly the ones you are after. November and December sell out well in advance, and the biggest groups book from September.

A simple rule: the larger the group, the earlier you must book, because you are not looking for a table but for a whole space to yourselves.

## 6. Keep everything in one place if you can

Every move breaks the momentum. The walk between the activity and the restaurant costs twenty minutes, two or three people who use it to head home, and a group that arrives scattered.

Venues that combine the activity, the food and the bar in one space avoid that dead time, and it is often what separates a good team building venue from a merely good activity. Our overview of formats is in [team building activities in Paris](/en/team-building-seminaires/).

## 7. Protect the informal time after the activity

This is the one most organisers learn the hard way. The activity creates the memories, but cohesion happens in the hour that follows, when people debrief, tease each other and finally talk about something other than work.

Always plan 45 minutes to an hour after the activity, with something to drink and snack on. If the budget is tight, cut elsewhere, not here.

## 8. Never force participation

Making the activity compulsory is the surest way to produce the opposite of what you want. There will always be someone who hates singing, playing, running or being the centre of attention.

The answer is not to excuse them and leave them aside, but to give them a genuine role: referee, timekeeper, photographer, or captain who organises without playing. They stay in the group, take part their own way, and nobody counts who dropped out.

## 9. Sort out the aftermath in advance

Photos, videos, posts on the company's social accounts. All of that is prepared beforehand, not in the excitement at the end of the night.

- Warn people that photos will be taken and leave an easy way out for those who decline.
- Appoint one person to take the photos, otherwise nobody does or everybody does it badly.
- Decide upfront what goes out externally and what stays internal.

## 10. Measure, but with two questions maximum

A thirty-item survey the next day gets ignored. Two questions sent the same day are enough to know whether you repeat the format or change it.

The two that work best:

- Would you recommend this activity to another team, yes or no?
- What would you change first next time?

The second one gives you all the material. Keep the answers from year to year, they are worth more than any provider comparison.

## What exactly is a team building?

A team building is an organised moment outside the usual work setting, bringing a team together around a shared activity to strengthen cohesion, communication and mutual understanding. It runs from one hour to a full day, in a leisure venue, a seminar space or the company's own offices.

What sets it apart from a regular team outing is the objective. An outing is enjoyable, a team building serves an identified intention, such as onboarding new joiners, connecting two departments or closing a heavy period. That is exactly why the first tip on this list matters more than all the others.

## The mistakes that come up most often

- Booking the activity before setting the objective, and ending up with a pleasant but pointless evening.
- Forgetting catering, and watching the group scatter at 7pm.
- Putting two departments in competition when the whole point was to bring them together.
- Planning a single activity for 40 people when only 6 can play at a time.
- Choosing a Friday night, the slot where attendance drops the most.
- Stacking three activities back to back with no breathing room, which exhausts everyone.

## Your questions

### What is a team building?

An organised moment outside the usual work setting, bringing a team together around a shared activity to strengthen cohesion, communication and mutual understanding. It lasts from an hour to a full day and differs from a simple outing by the goal it serves.

### How much does a team building cost per person?

Roughly 25 to 40 euros for a one to two hour activity alone, 50 to 80 euros with a meal or cocktails, and 100 euros and up for a full day with a venue buyout. Rates rise in November and December.

### How long should a team building last?

One to two hours of activity is enough if you keep informal time afterwards. Half a day suits a broader goal, a full day mainly makes sense when there is work content to cover as well.

### When should you book?

As early as possible for a weekday evening, the most sought-after slot. For November and December, allow several weeks, and start in September for a large group.

### Can a team building be made compulsory?

Attendance during working hours can be expected, but forcing active participation always backfires. Offer an alternative role, referee, photographer or timekeeper, rather than letting someone sit through it.
